The useful question is not “which chord means sad?” It is: which musical controls reliably move which part of an emotional experience?

A design model, not a claim that emotion is reducible to four knobs.

One distinction first

00

Music can sound angry without making you angry.

Strong conceptual distinction

Perceived emotion vs felt emotion

Perceived: the listener recognizes expression in the music, almost like recognizing anger in a voice. Felt: the listener’s own state changes. Philosophers such as Peter Kivy stressed that recognition does not require contagion; Juslin’s work asks which mechanisms actually turn sound into a felt response.

Design use

Build v1 to make a sentence legible as emotion. Do not promise to induce that emotion. Later, test felt effects separately with self-report or physiology.

Why this matters

A mapping can be excellent at communicating “grief” and still be pleasurable, fascinating, or emotionally neutral to hear.

Expectation is the engine

01

Emotion often lives less in a chord than in what the chord does to a prediction.

Huron’s ITPRA cycle
ImagineWhat might happen?
TensionPrepare for it.
PredictRight or wrong?
ReactFast body response.
AppraiseWas it good?
Strong theory, broad evidence

Meyer → Huron: expectation creates feeling over time

Meyer argued that musical emotion grows when a tendency is delayed, blocked, or fulfilled. Huron adds five moments: imagined outcomes, anticipatory tension, prediction accuracy, an immediate reaction, then slower appraisal. A surprise can feel startling at first and delightful a beat later. Frisson is especially tied to abrupt, expectation-breaking events: an entrance, leap, dynamic shock, or harmonic turn.

Design use

Tension should control trajectory, not chord labels. Increase delay before resolution, harmonic distance, syncopation, or a violated pattern. Let “relief” be the arrival after tension, not a static consonant sound.

For sequential text, this is gold: each message teaches the listener what to expect; the next message can fulfill, bend, or break it. The conversation itself becomes the memory the music predicts against.

There is no single pipe

02

Juslin’s BRECVEMA model says music reaches emotion through several routes. They need different controls.

Brainstem reflexSudden, loud, rough, or extreme sound triggers alert.
Rhythmic entrainmentYour body’s timing couples to pulse.
Evaluative conditioningA sound inherits feeling from repeated associations.
Emotional contagionSound imitates expressive voice or movement.
Visual imageryMusic evokes internal scenes and motion.
Episodic memoryA sound retrieves a personally charged event.
Musical expectancyEvents confirm or violate learned syntax.
Aesthetic judgmentTaste, skill, beauty, novelty, and goals shape appraisal.
Good framework; routes vary by listener

Mechanism before mapping

Tempo can work through bodily entrainment. A bright attack can resemble excited speech through contagion. A familiar timbre may work through memory. The same acoustic move can reach different listeners by different routes.

Design use

Tag every mapping rule with its intended route. For a shared instrument, lean on reflex, entrainment, vocal-like expression, and learned expectancy. Put personal memory and conditioning in an optional user-tuned layer.

Dissonance is partly in the spectrum

03

“Tense” is not a property of intervals alone. It depends on how the sound’s partials collide.

One interval, different timbres
partials align
partials beat

Nearby frequencies inside the ear’s critical bandwidth produce beating called roughness. Sethares shows that changing a timbre’s spectrum changes which tunings sound locally consonant. But roughness is not the whole story: style and expertise strongly affect whether dissonance is pleasant.

Strong psychoacoustics

Roughness is a controllable physical lever

Roughness reliably contributes to tension and alertness. Yet listeners can enjoy it, and harmonic context changes its meaning. “Consonance” combines sensory properties with learned syntax and style.

Design use

Use certainty/confidence → spectral stability: high confidence can mean aligned partials, stable tuning, fewer beating components; low confidence can mean detuning, inharmonicity, or tremulous modulation. Keep harmonic distance as a separate expectation control.

Mode works. It is not a law.

04

Major/minor valence is reliable inside familiar Western listening cultures and fragile outside them.

Mixed across cultures

Tempo carries arousal more safely than mode carries valence

Experiments repeatedly find that faster tempo raises perceived activation. Mode can shift positive/negative ratings, especially for enculturated listeners. But research with listeners in Papua New Guinea found negligible evidence for a universal major-happy/minor-sad effect. Cross-cultural work finds both shared cues and culture-specific learning.

Design use

Map arousal → tempo, event density, attack rate with higher confidence. Treat valence → mode as a learned preset, not the universal core. Pair valence with contour, articulation, timbre, and harmonic behavior.

Do not collapse axes

Fast minor can feel urgent or furious; slow major can feel tender or solemn. Tempo and mode are independent enough to preserve mixed emotions.

Timbre is emotional grammar

05

Before harmony arrives, a sound already has posture: sharp, breathy, hollow, heavy, intimate.

Useful acoustic handles
soft onset
hard onset
dark spectrum

stable tone
noisy tone
low register

Good experimental support

Brightness, attack, noise, and register carry affect

Timbre studies find repeatable links between acoustic features and words such as bright, happy, tense, sad, heroic, or scary, though pitch register interacts with instrument identity. Spectral centroid roughly tracks brightness; attack sharpness and high-frequency energy often raise activity or force.

Design use

Give valence and arousal multiple timbral outputs: brightness, attack, breath/noise, and register. Do not let “black” merely switch major to minor. It might darken the spectrum, lower the register, soften the onset, or narrow the sound.

Tonal tension can be modeled

06

Lerdahl turns “far from home” into a set of measurable musical distances.

Useful model; incomplete predictor

Hierarchy gives tension a shape

Tonal Pitch Space models tension through distance between chords and keys, attraction toward stable pitches, and the build/release pattern across a phrase. Listener studies support parts of the model, while performance, dynamics, timing, and individual hearing still matter.

Design use

Track a musical home for each conversation. Map emotional tension to distance from that home, prolongation away from it, and strength of return. This gives every message a relation to what came before.
